Batch 19 · the sub-tab layer

Sixteen pages that split themselves

Fifteen pages in the app carry a tab bar inside the page — around sixty states that were never drawn. Drawing them turned out to be less about layout than about a question the system had not answered: when is a strip of labels a set of sections, and when is it a filter, a view switcher, or a sub-nav that escaped? Only seven of the fifteen turned out to need a tab strip at all.

The strip sits between the stat strip and the toolbar. That ordering is the argument: the stats describe the whole page, the tabs choose the content, the toolbar filters what the tabs chose. Put the toolbar above the strip and its chips appear to apply to every tab at once.

The four tests · proposed for §07b
01It is a section if the content is different

Different columns, different shape, different primary action. Ceremony’s Processional and Reception Details share nothing but a page. Use a tab strip.

02It is a filter if the rows are the same, fewer

Floral / Bridal / Decor are one collection cut five ways. Gift type is one column. These go in the toolbar as chips, where an active one carries a ✕ and says how many rows it hid.

03It is a view if the data is the same, drawn differently

Month, Week, Agenda. Vertical Timeline and Day-Of Details. The system already has a view switcher on the right of the toolbar; that is where these belong.

04It is a page if it owns its own records

Covenant’s five have their own records, their own print class, their own primary action. They belong in the sub-nav with the other thirty-two, not behind a strip inside one.

—Two levels is the ceiling

Shot Lists and Share Packets earn a second strip. Nothing gets a third: at that depth the page is two pages wearing one name.

—Stack when the sections are one argument

Catering has nine sections and no tabs, because all nine read the same headcount. Tabs would hide eight of the nine consequences of changing one RSVP.

Group 1 · genuine sections

Seven pages that keep a tab strip

Different content behind each label — different columns, different shape, sometimes a different primary action. Ceremony is the deepest at nine, and the clearest: nobody wants Processional and Reception Details in one scroll. Note 19e, drawn immediately after 19d as the counter-example — the same page with its category tabs moved to where they belong.

19a
Ceremony & Reception · nine section tabs
The deepest tab set in the planner. Nine sections is too many to stack — the page would be four screens tall and the officiant would scroll past the thing they came for. The strip sits between the stat strip and the toolbar, which is the whole ordering argument: the stats describe the page, the tabs choose the content, the toolbar filters what the tabs chose. Put the toolbar above the tabs and its chips appear to apply to all nine.

Catering & Menu · nine sections, deliberately NOT tabbed
The page with the most sections in the planner, and the one that must not use tabs. Every section here reads the same headcount: change an RSVP and the menu covers, the children’s count, the place settings and the costs all move together. Tabs would hide eight of those nine consequences. Tabs are for sections that are independent; stacking is for sections that are one argument.
